Omnifold — Designing a System that Makes Origami a Credible Educational Tool

Omnifold

The Problem

Origami is widely dismissed as a children's craft — simple, decorative, and educationally marginal. This perception is both inaccurate and wasteful. Origami has proven applications in structural engineering, aerospace (NASA has used folding principles for satellite solar panels), mathematics, psychology, and art therapy. The barrier is not origami's potential — it is the design of how origami is introduced, contextualised, and integrated into education.

The brief asked: how do you design a system that repositions origami from a children's pastime to a credible, curriculum-integrated educational tool for students aged 6 to 17?

My Role

This was a system design project requiring work across multiple scales — from the organisational structure of a new educational institution to the design of physical and digital touchpoints that students and teachers would interact with. I was responsible for the full design scope: stakeholder mapping, user journey design, brand identity, publication design (age-differentiated student booklets), event design, website wireframes, and the overall system architecture.

Reframing the Problem

"The problem isn't that origami lacks educational value. The problem is that no one has designed the system through which that value reaches students and teachers."

The existing educational curriculum had identifiable gaps that origami could directly address: lack of interdisciplinary approaches, insufficient practical/hands-on learning, rote learning emphasis over critical thinking, and a lack of multisensory learning experiences. These gaps became the design brief's anchor points — Omnifold needed to be designed as the solution to each of them.

Stakeholder Mapping & System Architecture

Omnifold is not a product — it is an institution. Delivering origami as a credible educational tool requires a network of stakeholders working in coordination: founders, academic departments, curriculum designers, workshop facilitators (origamists), school administrators, parents, educational material providers, and the students themselves.

I mapped this entire ecosystem — from the core team structure through to the operational departments (finance, legal, public relations, operations) — to understand where design touchpoints could intervene to make the system function. The student sits at the centre of concentric rings of influence; every design decision was evaluated by asking whether it served the learner's journey.

User Journey: The Student Experience

The student journey through Omnifold was mapped across three phases:

  • Before (Awareness → Interest → Desire): Introduced through a magazine/booklet that frames origami's possibilities. Generating interest through simple introductory activities and visually appealing materials. Student approaches parents with the desire to enrol.
  • During (Action → Involvement → Deeper Interest → Sense of Accomplishment): Receiving the programme schedule and materials kit. Participating in activities and team exercises. Connecting the origami techniques to subjects in the academic curriculum — STEM, mathematics, art, psychology. Developing a sense of progress and achievement.
  • After (Recognition): Willingness to participate in events and competitions. Building community connections. Gaining exposure to industry experts. Becoming an ambassador for origami as a legitimate learning practice.

Design Deliverables

The Omnifold system required design work across four distinct output areas:

  • Brand Identity: Visual identity, logo, business card, identity cards, brochure, merchandise, stationery, iconography — all anchored in the origami aesthetic while projecting professional credibility.
  • Publications: Two age-differentiated student booklets (ages 6–10 and ages 11–17), a camp schedule, and programme mockups — designed to engage different developmental stages while maintaining brand consistency.
  • Event Design: Space design, wayfinding, invitation posters, collateral, infographics, and iconography for the summer camp environment.
  • Website: Iconography, digital mockups, wireframes, and website banners for the institutional web presence.

The Core Design Insight

"The system's credibility comes not from the origami itself but from how the origami is framed — every touchpoint from the booklet to the certificate communicates that this is serious, valued learning."

Every design decision reinforced institutional credibility. The publications use clear typographic hierarchy and considered white space rather than the visual noise of typical children's craft materials. The brand identity avoids primary colours and cartoon aesthetics in favour of a system that would not look out of place in a professional educational context. The camp certificate and completion recognition were designed with the same care as the programme itself — because the student's sense of achievement is as much a design problem as the curriculum.

Outcome & Reflection

Omnifold demonstrated that changing perception is fundamentally a design challenge. Origami's credibility problem was not a communication problem that better marketing could solve — it was a system design problem requiring every touchpoint to tell a consistent story about value and seriousness.

This project was my first experience working at true system scale — designing not just individual artefacts but the relationships between them. It reinforced my interest in the intersection of interaction design and service design, and shaped how I now think about any design brief: starting not with the artefact but with the ecosystem it needs to function within.
